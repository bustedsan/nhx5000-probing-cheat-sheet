Where AI Hits Our Shop

Digital AI Exposure, scored 0–10, for the roles in this room. The logic is simple: the more a job’s work product is a digital file made at a screen — a model, a drawing, a program, an analysis — the higher the exposure. The more a job is hands-on in the real world, the lower it scores.

Scoring rubric and physical-vs-digital logic from Andrej Karpathy’s US Job Market Visualizer (he ran AI at Tesla and co-founded OpenAI), applied here to our specific aerospace/defense machine-shop roles.

Lower exposure (hands-on) Higher exposure (digital)
AI is not going to replace engineers. Engineers who use AI are going to replace the ones who don’t.
A high score does not mean the job disappears — it means the job changes. Software developers score 9 and that field is still projected to grow. Exposure means reshaped, not replaced. The knowledge in this room — why a 13-8 part fails, what a probe cycle should feel like, a good setup from a scrap part — is exactly what makes these tools powerful in our hands.

These are rough, defensible estimates calibrated to the same 0–10 anchors Karpathy uses (roofer/diver 0–1; electrician/plumber 2–3; nurse/police 4–5; teacher/accountant 6–7; software developer/drafter 8–9; data-entry clerk 10). They do not account for demand, regulation, or preference for human workers — they estimate how much AI will reshape the work, not whether the job goes away.
